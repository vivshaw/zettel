- # 1 - The Idea of Perfection
	- ()chapter also available online here: https://yalereview.org/article/iris-murdoch-perfection)
	- in a sense, philosophy is always trying to return to the beginning. that's not necessarily a bad thing. and this chapter will attempt such a return
	- how is it that an unexamined life can be moral? why is [[love]] a central concept in morality? contemporary ethicists talk a lot about [[consciousness]] and [[freedom]]... where do these facts fit in?
	- starts with [[G.E. Moore]], who is both the beginning of the contemporary school of thought Murdoch addresses, and now unthinkable within that school. he separated "what does good mean?" from "what things are good?" but also thought that the good was real, suprasensible, and out there in the world. and thought of the good as analogous to beauty - "to see it is, in some sense, to have it
	- the trend Murdoch addresses is of "good" as a function of the will. on this view, "what things are good?" cannot be answered, because it's a matter of judgments of the will and choices of the individual. less like vision, more like movement.
	- does a tour through [[Stuart Hampshire]]'s *Thought and Action* and *Disposition and Memory* as an examplar of the trend. "We should abandon the image of man as a detached observer, and should rather picture him as an object moving among other objects in a continuaal flow of intention into action"
		- the inner world has "a parasitic and shadowy nature"
		- thought and belief are separate from will and action. will is wholly personal, reason is wholly impersonal. the inner life is not a moral sphere.
		- [[intention]] is the core, and is conscious
		- writes about "the ideally rational man", though recognizes we are not ideally rational due to our many dispositions. thinks rationality would leave us without art or dream
		- > We ought to know what we are doing. We should aim at total knowledge of our situation and a clear conceptualization of all our possibilities. Thought and intention must be directed toward definite overt issues or else they are merely day-dream. 'Reality' is potentially open to different observers. What is 'inward', what lies between overt actions, is either impersonal thought, or 'shadows' of actions, or else substanceless dream. Mental life is, and must be, a shadow of life in public. Our personal being is the movement of our overtly choosing will.
		- on this view, morality is like visiting a shop. i enter, i objectively estimate the quality and purpose of the various goods, and i select the ones that best meet my needs.
		- > Modern ethics analyzes 'good', the empty action word which is the correlate of the isolated will, and tends to ignore other value terms
		- this view is in a sense behaviourist (in that it connects the meaning of an action to what's publicly observable), existentialist (in that it removes the substantial self and puts the single solitary will foremost), and utilitarian (in that morality is only about public actions and effects)
		- it is also democratic- morality is something any ordinary man does.
	- detour down the history of [[sense-data]]. are there private appearances of which we have infallible knowledge? from [[Descartes]] until recently, most philosophers thought so. but nowadays, many argue that "there's no use" and "it's not there". the possesion of the knowledge "red" is a public skill- i stop at the light when it turns, etc.
	- isn't there such a thing as deciding and not acting? no! "a wheel that can be turned while affecting nothing else is not part of the mechanism". [[Wittgenstein]] as the trigger of such thinking
	- Murdoch's counterexample: what about a mother-in-law M who hates her daughter-in-law D, but is too prim and proper to do or say anything about it? she treats her perfectly, yet regards her with utter contempt and hates being in her presence. is that comprehensible in this picture? then, let's imagine that over time M gradually comes to see what her son sees in D, and comes to like her after all, all while changing nothing in her behavior. did anything happen?
		- where's the room for struggle or change?
		- consider the use of [[thick]] moral terms: she goes from seeing D as "bumptious" to seeing her as "gay"
		- M is trying to see D justly or lovingly. this is a progressive act! trying over time to see another correctly.
		- introducing the idea of progress introduces also the idea of perfection, making the task endless
		- accounts like this are common in novels, and none of us would fail to understand them. so Murdoch's view is certainly at least comprehensible.
	- > Hume and Kant, the two patron saints of modern philosophy, abhor history, each in his own way, and abhor the particular notion of privacy which history entails.
	- once the historical individual is let in, our perception has to change. one's reality is about the progressing life of a person.
	- Hampshire tries to use psychoanalysis and "the ideal analyst" to cover for this... but why should some analyst be the be-all and end-all?
	- Murdoch wants to connect the idea of the individual and the idea of perfection. moral tasks are endless not just because our efforts are imperfect, but because our concepts change with us.
	- this is not so dissimilar from [[Plato]], nor the ethic of [[Christianity]]. or: that moral terms are concrete universals.
	- [[Kant]] suggested that when confronted with the person of Christ, we must turn back to our own reason and decide whether we approve of what we see. this is not an existentialist turn, where it's up to our will- Kant's a moral naturalist. rather: perhaps it's a matter of "tactics and temperament" whether we look outward or inward.
	- > Where virtue is concerned we often apprehend more than we understand, and grow by _looking_.
	- moral progress is a sort of deepening or complicating. in the "siege of the individual by concepts", we learn and use the secondary moral value concepts - "vulgar", "spontaneous", etc.\
	- aesthetic language as an analogy- an art critic can help us to understand more about a painting, if we both speak the same language. he can help us see what he sees. and we can't necessarily understand a single term without understanding sets or patterns- we need to know what he values to understand what he's saying.
	- > Progress in understanding of a scheme of concepts often takes place as we listen to normative-desscriptive talk in the presence of a common object.
	- moral language is often unavoidably idiosyncratic and inaccessible, and that's ok! we are obscure to one another and words are subtle things.
	- > Words are the most subtle symbols which we possess and our human fabric depends on them. The living and radical nature of language is something which we forget at our peril. It is totally misleading to speak, for instance, of “two cultures,” one literary-humane and the other scientific, as if these were of equal status. There is only one culture, of which science, so interesting and so dangerous, is now an important part. But the most essential and fundamental aspect of culture is the study of literature, since this is an education in how to picture and understand human situations. We are men and we are moral agents before we are scientists, and the place of science in human life must be discussed in words. This is why it is and always will be more important to know about Shakespeare than to know about any scientist: and if there is a “Shakespeare of science” his name is Aristotle.
	- use of [[attention]], borrowed from [[Simone Weil]], to mean a just and loving gaze directed at an individual reality. Murdoch believes this to be the characteristic (logical claim) and proper (normative claim) mark of an active moral agent.
	- do the existentialists have an advantage? here's a particular feature of moral choice: emptiness that often occurs at the moment of choosing. does this mean our choices are not related to our reasons?
	- attention as the _via media_ between the empty will and determinism. i can only choose within the world i can see, where moral vision requires moral imagination and effort. i might be compelled automatically by what i see. but what i see was determined by my past efforts. the moral life goes on continually. it's not switched off between choices. progress can be obscure, historically conditioned, and not always conscious
	- > I would suggest that at the level of serious common sense and of an ordinary non-philosophical reflection about the nature of morals it is perfectly obvious that goodness is connected with knowledge: not with impersonal quasi-scientific knowledge of the ordinary world, whatever that may be, but with a refined and honest perception of what is really the case, a patient and just discernment and exploration of what confronts one, which is the result not simply of opening one’s eyes but of a certainly perfectly familiar kind of moral discipline.
	- > The place of choice is certainly a different one if we think in terms of a world which is compulsively present to the will, and the discernment and exploration of which is a slow business. Moral change and moral achievement are slow; we are not free in the sense of being able suddenly to alter ourselves since we cannot suddenly alter what we can see and ergo what we desire and are compelled by. In a way, explicit choice seems now less important: less decisive (since much of the “decision” lies elsewhere) and less obviously something to be “cultivated.” If I attend properly I will have no choices and this is the ultimate condition to be aimed at.
	- will is _not_ separate from reason. it influences our belief. from Weil, "will is obedience not resolution".
	- > Virtue is au fond the same in the artist as in the good man in that it is a selfless attention to nature: something which is easy to name but very hard to achieve. Artists who have reflected have frequently given expression to this idea. (For instance, Rilke praising Cézanne speaks of a “consuming of love in anonymous work.” Letter to Clara Rilke, October 13, 1907.)
	- >  Good is indefinable not for the reasons offered by Moore’s successors, but because of the infinite difficulty of the task of apprehending a magnetic but inexhaustible reality.
	- references George Herbert's ["The Elixir"](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/44362/the-elixir) - "Who sweeps a room as for Thy laws makes that and th' action fine." #poetry
	- this picture isn't meant to be a neutral logical analysis of morality, nor a practical guide to be applied in every moral situation. it is "a kind of inconclusive non-dogmatic naturalism".
	- > Hampshire said, and I quoted this at the start, that “it is the constructive task of the philosophy of mind to provide a set of terms in which ultimate judgements of value can be very clearly stated.” I would put what I think is much the same task in terms of the provision of rich and fertile conceptual schemes which help us to reflect upon and understand the nature of moral progress and moral failure and the reasons for the divergence of one moral temperament from another.
